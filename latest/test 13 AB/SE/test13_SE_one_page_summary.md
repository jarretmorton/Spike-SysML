# Test 13 (SE Arm) — One-Page Summary

**Task.** Drive the rover straight at a wall (~1 m away) at maximum speed and stop as close as possible without touching it. Two hard constraints: *run at maximum speed* and *no contact*. The arm followed a gated systems-engineering process — analyze, model, calibrate, verify, then five scored operation runs — touching hardware only after the analysis was done. This campaign ran on Claude Opus 5 under SE framework v2 — incognito session, web search off, maximum effort, thinking on auto.

**Result.** All five operation runs stopped with no contact, **19–27 mm** from the wall (mean **22.4 mm**, operator-measured), at full speed. The core win: predict-then-verify caught three defects before any scored run, two of them contact — one by checking its own paperwork before the first flash, two by verification runs that falsified written predictions. The arm's own estimates ran 2.8 mm high on every run, a bias toward the wall whose direction it committed to in writing beforehand.

**What went wrong, and how it was corrected:**

- **The rover drove in a circle, and the sensor lost the wall.** Calibration run 1 commanded both motors to "maximum," sending each to *its own* ceiling; they differ by 6.2%, so the rover tracked a 2 m arc, 25° of turn over a full approach. An ultrasonic sensor facing a flat wall stops hearing its echo past 10–15° off-square, so the wall vanished mid-run and three impossible readings followed. Fix: **both wheels at one common regulated speed** below the slower motor's ceiling, plus a closed-loop heading hold whose gain was *derived* from the measured arc, not guessed. Drift fell under 2°. "Maximum speed" now means maximum *straight-line* speed, 4.6% slower — raised as a requirement change and approved, not absorbed quietly.

- **A faulty forward sensor, found by disagreement rather than suspicion.** The arm cross-checked all three rangers against each other and against wheel travel at every pose; the secondary forward sensor read 106–693 mm off. It was failed against its requirement and demoted to a logged monitor, kept rather than deleted so the failure stays visible. The rear sensor, permanently at its maximum because it faces an open room, taught the second lesson: **a saturated reading is a state, not a distance.** Feeding that maximum to the estimator had killed one calibration attempt. Afterwards every consumer of a range rejects such readings, and sensors are identified by *which one saturates* rather than by port order — the original scheme would have inverted every distance decision.

- **Two constants bound on reasoning rather than measurement — each falsified a frozen prediction.** The sensor's staleness was argued from physics — data cannot be much older than one reading interval — giving 22 ms against a true 66, and the rover stopped 19 mm closer than commanded, contact at the target then planned. The offset between what the sensor reports and where the front corner sits was measured 8° off square, because calibration reached that pose only after manoeuvres had turned it, while operation squares up and stays within 1° — making that correction 8 mm of pure error. Neither showed in any single channel; the first surfaced when two independent estimates of one stop disagreed by 20 mm. Both were fixed by re-measuring in the operating configuration. The arm's own verdict: it enforced "test like you fly" on the program, then broke it in the measurements — the harder half to see.

- **The rover cannot measure its own gap below about 23 mm — and the fix missed by 1 mm.** Near its floor the forward sensor stops reporting usefully, so at a tight gap the independent check goes blind. Worse, the onboard estimate proved arithmetically **circular** — expanded out it equals the commanded target plus about a millimetre, so it cannot detect a stopping error at all. The arm raised the target from 14 to **26 mm deliberately — buying visibility, not margin** — and declared the reporting requirement FAILED rather than close it on a meaningless number. The real floor proved 4 mm above the datasheet, so one run in five got a clear reading — accurate to 0.26 mm against 3.4 mm for the four on the floor. That comparison is the evidence the requirement was correctly failed.

- **Telemetry and harness cost.** Readings are buffered during motion and sent only after the wheels stop, so the radio can never delay the stop decision; computed results go out before raw data, which saved the third verification run's numbers when its dump was cut short. Two costs remained: raw traces stayed on the host, unreachable, so figures were rebuilt from transcribed values; and re-sending the same unchanged program before each of five power-cycled runs consumed enough budget that later runs got one-line summaries rather than individual analysis.

**Stopping-algorithm changes (top level).** The stop command — a hard brake on both motors — never changed. What decides it changed once: from a distance trigger on assumed constants to one on measured constants, with the wall's position continuously re-fixed in wheel-travel terms so the slow sensor sets the target and the fast wheel count times the brake. For the first fast run the trigger came off the sensor entirely onto a fixed travel limit: the worst case over the unmeasured assumptions was 570 mm of stopping distance on a 1000 mm runway, so no sensor-triggered target was safe. After that only three constants and the commanded gap changed: sensor delay, corner offset, target. The arithmetic was never adjusted to make a result come out right.

**What held by luck.** Less than usual, but not none. The first calibration run's arc curved *away* from the wall; mirrored, it would have driven the rover in at full speed with the sensor blind. Both systematics — the 19 mm delay bias and the 8 mm angle error — pointed outward; either reversed was contact before anything was measured. And the final stops landed 3.6 mm closer than commanded — a bias toward the wall, harmless only because the target had been raised to 26 mm for an unrelated reason.

**Score against the criteria.**
1. Characterization/verification runs (fewer better): **5** — 2 calibration runs (eight phases each, one traverse per run) and 3 verification runs, each testing a prediction the previous falsified — vs a 1-run plan.
2. Human interventions (fewer better): **2** — one gap measurement after calibration, one after the first verification run, held to the 2-measurement plan. **0 impacts.**
3. Operation runs with no contact (more better): **5 / 5**.
4. Closeness of stops: **19–27 mm, mean 22.4, best 19, sd 3.13 mm**.
5. Predicted vs actual error: **22.4 − 26.0 = −3.6 mm**; per-run estimates ran **+2.8 mm high on all five** (sd 1.9).
