# Test 11 (SE Arm) — One-Page Summary

**Task.** Drive the rover straight at a wall (~1 m away) at maximum speed and stop as close as possible without touching it. Two hard constraints: *run at maximum speed* and *no contact*. The arm followed a gated systems-engineering process — analyze, model, calibrate, verify, then five scored operation runs — touching hardware only after the analysis was done. This was a repeat of an earlier identical setup with only the underlying model changed, to see whether that changes the result.

**Result.** All five operation runs stopped with **no contact**, but far from the wall: **143–176 mm, mean 160 (operator-measured)**, against a frozen prediction of about **41 mm**. The stops were safe but not close. The real story is what the process caught and what it didn't: predict-then-verify exposed a hidden steering fault at a gate check that the arm's own numbers had missed, and after the runs it explained a large closeness miss down to the millimetre. The durable win was **auditability, not closeness** — every stop landed where the (flawed) calibration said it would, and the flaw was traceable rather than mysterious.

**What went wrong, and how it was corrected:**

- **Calibration took two flights, not one.** The first calibration run aborted safely without moving toward the wall: the rover couldn't tell its motors apart — they're mounted mirror-image, so the "both forward" test spun it in place — and by the time it retried, it had turned far enough that all its sensors lost the wall. The arm rebuilt the start-up routine (undo any rotation before the next step; use stronger, heading-corrected nudges) and the second flight calibrated cleanly. Two calibration flights against a one-flight plan — but both failures surfaced in a safe abort, not a crash.

- **A mismatched ultrasonic sensor, caught before moving.** At the very first standstill reading the two front sensors disagreed by 118 mm — impossible against a flat wall — so one was mounted well outside spec. The badly behaved sensor (slow, dropping out at speed, garbage at close range) was **demoted to a backup/diagnostic** and the cleaner one made primary, with glitch filtering: median-of-three readings, a confirm step, and wheel dead-reckoning to bridge dropouts. The twist: in operation the *demoted* sensor was actually the closer-to-truth one, and a built-in "the two channels disagree" flag said so on every run — but neither sensor carried the real answer, so the flag warned correctly without being usable mid-run.

- **No steering at first — and a decode error that hid it.** The rover originally drove both wheels equally and never corrected heading. Its motors top out about 6% apart at full speed, which arced it roughly **39°** off straight — but in analysis the arm misread its own heading log by a factor of ten and reported ~3.9°, then checked the wrong statistic. **The operator rejected the gate check on the steering requirement** — the single most valuable outside input in the run. The arm owned the error, added an **active steering trim** (hold the outer wheel at full speed, ease the wheel that's leading the turn), re-verified, and passed on resubmission; the trim cut the heading error to well under 1° in normal driving, with the small remaining swing coming from the brake itself, inside limits.

- **A telemetry dump that dropped the safety marker.** On the good calibration flight the wireless link ran ~6× slower than the previous session, so the data dump overran its time limit and lost its tail — including the end-of-run "sentinel" that proves the program shut down cleanly (the safety-critical byte was last in line). No calibration data was lost. The fix sized the operation dump small enough to finish even on the slowest link, and reordered priorities so the sentinel can't be the casualty.

- **Why the gap stayed so large.** The single tape measurement used to correct the sensors was taken with the rover **sitting ~28° off square**, left over from the calibration maneuvers, rather than pointed straight at the wall. At that angle the sensor's echo off the flat wall behaves differently than when driving straight, so the correction computed there didn't transfer. Driving straight, the primary sensor read ~100 mm short of what the correction assumed, so **the rover believed the wall was ~100 mm closer than it was, braked early, and stopped ~160 mm out instead of ~41**. The error made the rover think it was *closer*, never farther — so it always stopped short of the wall, never into it.

**Stopping-algorithm changes (top level).** Unlike earlier tests, the stop trigger started where those tests *ended*: it fires on the corrected front-sensor gap, projected forward by the wheels between good readings and filtered for glitches, so a stalled sensor reading can't cause a late stop. The stop command (a firm motor brake) and that trigger **never changed** across the whole campaign. The one substantive change came after the gate rejection: **active steering trim was added to the approach** to hold the rover straight. So the decision of *when* to stop and *how* to stop was stable; only *how straight it drove there* was fixed.

**What held by luck, and what didn't.** The no-contact result was **earned by design, not luck** — the whole scheme is built to fail early and put every plausible error on the safe (farther-out) side, and it did, even against an error ~15× larger than the margin budgeted for it. But that is the uncomfortable part: the arm **did not know** the stops would land at 160 mm; it predicted 41. Safety came from the design's built-in caution, not from the arm's knowledge of the sensor error — that error was only pinned *after* the five runs, by the five tape measurements. Closeness was the price paid for that caution.

**Score against the criteria.**
1. Characterization/verification runs (fewer better): **4** — 2 calibration flights (one a safe abort) + 2 verification flights (one gate rejection) — vs a 2-run plan.
2. Human interventions (fewer better): **2** — the M1 tape measurement (taken at calibration run 2) and the operator's gate-check rejection on steering — vs a 1-measurement plan.
3. Operation runs with no contact (more better): **5 / 5** (zero contact, ground-truth confirmed).
4. Closeness of stops: **143–176 mm, mean 160, best 143, standard deviation 14.6 mm**.
5. Predicted vs actual: **160 mm − 41 mm = +119 mm** — stops landed ~120 mm farther than predicted; the run-to-run spread, though, was predicted almost exactly (14.6 mm measured vs ~13 mm predicted).
