# Test 11 (Freestyle Arm) — One-Page Summary

**Task.** Drive the rover straight at a wall (~1 m away) at maximum speed and stop as close as possible without touching it. Two hard constraints: *run at maximum speed* and *no contact*. Unlike the systems-engineering arm, this arm was given no prescribed method — free to develop and calibrate however it judged best, then lock one program and run it five times. This campaign ran on Claude Fable 5 under the freestyle prompt — incognito session, web search off, maximum effort, thinking on auto.

**Result.** All five operation runs stopped with **no contact**, but the stops split sharply in two: **28 mm on one run and 131–140 mm on the other four** (operator-measured). It got there in **seven characterization runs** — three of which deliberately drove into the wall to find the sensor offset — with **no discretionary operator measurements**. The 28 mm run shows the control law can stop close; the wide spread on the other four was the primary sensor feeding false readings, not the control law and not anything in the lane.

**What went wrong, and how it was corrected:**

- **Three calibration runs that hit the wall on purpose.** To find how far the sensor sits from the bumper without a tape measurement, the arm crept the rover slowly into the wall until the wheels stalled — a gentle contact — and read the offset from the stall point. It took three tries (calibration runs 2–4): an early creep, one that veered ~35° off straight and failed, and a gyro-steered creep that pinned sensor A's offset at ~9.5 mm (locked conservatively at 12 mm). The operation runs made none. The arm chose to spend three deliberate wall contacts rather than request a single ground-truth measurement.

- **A faulty forward sensor — and a primary that wasn't reliable either.** The two forward sensors disagreed by 120–230 mm at the start line, so the arm demoted B (which looked aimed off the wall at range) and made A its primary channel. Defensible from the calibration data — but A was not dependable in operation either: on four of five runs A read ~200 mm short at the start (while the demoted B read the wall correctly), then dropped to a false near value in flight. At the close-range stops the honest instrument was actually B (rock-steady, ~+10 mm); A caught oblique echoes and read long, so the committed A-only estimates ran high. Fusing or even cross-checking the two would have caught the disagreement and cut the estimate error to ~±3 mm.

- **Yaw — actively steered.** The arm closed the loop on heading: it held the outer wheel at full speed and eased the inner in proportion to the gyro's heading error, keeping the approach straight. The only residual was the brake itself — reverse-torque braking skids the chassis and briefly loses steering, leaving each stop yawed ~3–4°, which added a small ~4–6 mm corner-lead bias to the estimate.

- **Telemetry challenges — largely designed out.** The arm kept the sensor out of the fast approach loop and emitted only every ~100 ms, so the trigger loop never stalled. The hiccups were minor: the first flash timed out because the hub had gone to sleep (retried, no data lost), and after the runs the telemetry store expired before archiving, so a few charts were rebuilt from saved summaries.

- **Why the gap varied so much — and a wrong diagnosis.** The five stops were bimodal — 28 mm once, ~135 mm four times — and the cause was the primary sensor, not the environment. On four runs sensor A returned a false short reading at the start (~810 mm against a ~1 m wall), then a false near reading in flight that tripped the 60 mm safety cutoff, braking the rover early at ~135 mm. The odometry there (~850 mm travelled) shows the rover was still ~150 mm from the wall — not beside anything. On the one run where A read the wall correctly, it ran to its true trigger and stopped at 28 mm. The arm's own final report attributed the four wide stops to a physical obstacle in the lane — there was none; it mistook its primary sensor's false readings for an object.

**Stopping-algorithm changes (top level).** The stop command — a hard reverse-torque **motor brake** — never changed; what changed was what *decides* it. (1) Range the wall once while stationary, then fly on **wheel-encoder odometry alone**, braking when the odometry says the remaining gap hits the trigger (~52 mm ≈ braking + margin), gyro steering holding heading — the architecture follows from run 1's finding that the sonar is too stale to trust in motion. (2) Add an in-flight **tripwire** (brake at once if a fresh reading drops below a threshold) as a backstop; first set at 160 mm, but a validation run showed it preempted the real trigger through staleness, so it was tightened to 60 mm — this is the layer that caught the primary sensor's false readings in operation. (3) After a validation run showed the start ranging could latch onto a false short value, clamp the start reading to a sane window. A safe-side property runs through all of it: every error mode — stale reading, glitch, dropout, wheel slip — makes the rover brake *earlier* and stop *farther*, never later.

**What held by luck.** No-contact was mostly earned by design: every error mode pushes the stop farther from the wall, and the 60 mm tripwire turned the primary sensor's false readings into early stops rather than bad ones. The thin part was the one close stop — the 28 mm run ran on dead-reckoning from the standstill range with no in-flight check, safe only because the odometry, braking, and that one sensor read all held on that placement, with no backstop against a late stop if they hadn't. Closeness rested on a single clean reading, not on anything verified in advance.

**Score against the criteria.**
1. Characterization/verification runs (fewer better): **7** — 4 calibration (three of which deliberately hit the wall to find the offset) + 3 verification (each caught a real defect: tripwire preemption, false-latching start ranging, then a clean final flight) — no run budget set in advance.
2. Human interventions (fewer better): **0** — no discretionary measurements during characterization; the arm relied on the wall-stall contacts and the sensor floor instead (operation gaps were measured at close-out, for scoring only).
3. Operation runs with no contact (more better): **5 / 5** (the primary sensor misread on four of them; the safety cutoff caught it).
4. Closeness of stops: **28 mm on the clean run, 131–140 mm on the other four; mean 114, best 28.** The spread is bimodal — the primary sensor misreading, not control-law scatter.
5. Predicted vs actual error: The average actual gap - the pre operation estimate: **measured mean 113.6 − estimate 30 = 83.6 mm** — the estimate ran high, i.e. the rover judged itself farther out than it was.
